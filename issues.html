01

Technology & Innovation

Build the future in America — openly.

The countries that build the future get to live in it first. America should treat connectivity, compute, and publicly funded research the way we once treated highways and the power grid: as shared infrastructure that belongs to everyone. That means wiring every community, writing clear rules for artificial intelligence before it writes them for us, and making sure the public actually owns the breakthroughs it pays for.

The Plan

  • Gigabit for all 50 states by 2030. Treat high-speed internet as public infrastructure with a universal-service guarantee, open-access middle-mile fiber, and price floors that prevent monopoly gouging in rural and low-income areas.
  • A public, enforceable AI Bill of Rights. The right to know when a decision about you (a loan, a job, a benefit) was made by an algorithm; the right to a human appeal; and the right to your own data — including a real ability to delete it.
  • Open-by-default research. If taxpayers fund the science, taxpayers own the results. Publications, datasets, and patents from federally funded research become public goods, not private rents.
  • A $1 trillion Future Fund. A decade-long, scored commitment to basic science, advanced manufacturing, biotech, energy, and space — the moonshots private markets won't finance alone.
  • Independent, well-paid public-sector engineers. A digital service corps that builds government systems that work — benefits portals, tax filing, permitting — instead of renting failure from contractors.

How it works

Who builds the fiber

Federal grants seed open-access networks owned by states, co-ops, and municipalities; private ISPs lease capacity on equal terms. Public money, public conduit, competitive retail.

~$90B one-time

How AI rules are enforced

A dedicated office audits high-impact automated systems, publishes its findings, and can levy fines. Rules are written as plain-language standards, not 900-page rulebooks.

~$4B / yr

How "open by default" pays off

Open results compound: every lab can build on the last. We keep narrow national-security carve-outs, but the default flips from locked to open.

Net positive

Where the Future Fund goes

Allocated by independent boards on 10-year horizons, insulated from the annual budget knife, with every grant and outcome posted publicly.

~$100B / yr

02

Economy & Jobs

Work that pays. A life you can afford.

Four bills break most families: housing, childcare, healthcare, and energy. Bring each one down — with more supply, more competition, and hard caps only where markets clearly fail — and a middle-class life comes back within reach. At the same time, re-shore the industries that matter so the good jobs are here, and make starting a business something an ordinary person can actually do.

The Plan

  • Build 5 million homes. A federal–local partnership that rewards cities for legalizing housing (a "YIMBY" grant), funds the trades to build it, and unlocks public land for mixed-income development.
  • A "Build It Here" credit. Targeted tax credits for small and mid-sized manufacturers in critical sectors — chips, batteries, medicine, steel — tied to good wages and clean plants.
  • Portable benefits. Health, retirement, and paid leave that attach to the worker, not the job — so gig workers, freelancers, and job-switchers aren't left exposed.
  • Lower the four big bills. More housing supply, capped childcare costs, transparent healthcare pricing, and cheaper clean energy — measured as a real cut to the typical family's monthly budget.
  • A Postal Bank for the basics. Zero-fee starter checking and small-business accounts at every post office, ending predatory check-cashing in banking deserts.

How it works

Why a federal–local housing deal

Washington can't zone your town — but it can pay towns that say yes to housing and stop subsidizing those that block it. Money follows permits issued.

~$40B / yr

Wages as a condition

The manufacturing credit scales with pay and safety: firms that pay well and build clean get the full benefit; bad actors get nothing.

~$35B / yr

How portable benefits attach

Every employer and platform contributes pro-rata to a worker's portable account. Switch jobs, keep coverage. No more "benefits cliff."

Employer-funded

What the Postal Bank costs

It uses 30,000 post offices that already exist. Modest startup cost, self-sustaining on small interchange and treasury services.

~$2B startup

Across the aisle

Republicans

Typical view

Cheer re-shoring and cutting red tape on housing, and like that builders — not bureaucrats — do the building. Wary of new spending and of a Postal Bank they see as government overreach into banking.

Democrats

Typical view

Embrace portable benefits, childcare caps, and the housing push. Some want stronger union requirements and direct public housing rather than incentives to private developers.

Independents

Typical view

Focused on the bottom line: does my rent, daycare, and grocery bill actually fall? Supportive if the plan is paid for and the results are tracked, skeptical of anything that smells like a giveaway.

Rob's take

The fastest way to cut costs is to build — and that takes both parties' instincts. Deregulate housing like a conservative; protect the worker like a progressive. The deal is simple: we make it dramatically easier to build and produce here, and in exchange we attach wage and safety standards so the gains reach workers, not just balance sheets.

03

Climate & Energy

Clean power, cheaper bills, real jobs.

A clean grid isn't a sacrifice — it's the cheapest, most reliable power we've ever known, and millions of good jobs wiring it together. The plan is an all-of-the-above build-out: nuclear, solar, wind, geothermal, and storage; the transmission lines to move that power; and the home upgrades that cut every family's energy bill. We treat climate as an industrial opportunity, not a guilt trip.

The Plan

  • A clean grid by 2035. An all-of-the-above standard — advanced nuclear, solar, wind, geothermal, and storage — that prizes reliability and low cost, not any single technology.
  • A National Grid Corps. Permit and build the high-voltage transmission lines that connect cheap clean power to the cities that need it — the single biggest bottleneck to lower bills.
  • Cut every family's energy bill. Heat-pump, insulation, and weatherization rebates that lower monthly costs immediately, prioritized for working families.
  • Permitting reform — both ways. Make it fast to build clean energy and the transmission to carry it, while keeping real environmental review where it matters.
  • Restore the land. Plant a billion native trees, restore 100 million acres of habitat, and pay farmers and ranchers to be partners in carbon and conservation.

How it works

Why "all of the above"

A reliable clean grid needs firm power (nuclear, geothermal) and cheap variable power (solar, wind) and storage. Betting on one technology is how you get blackouts or high bills.

Mostly private capital

The transmission bottleneck

We have cheap power stuck where nobody lives. The Grid Corps fast-tracks interstate lines the way the interstate highways were built — national priority, federal siting.

~$30B / yr

Rebates that pay back

A heat pump can cut a household's heating bill substantially. Up-front rebates remove the cost barrier; the savings are permanent.

~$25B / yr

Farmers as partners

Conservation and carbon payments give rural America a new revenue stream — voluntary, paid, and locally run, not mandated from D.C.

~$10B / yr

Across the aisle

Republicans

Typical view

Like nuclear, permitting reform, and an "all of the above" approach that doesn't ban fossil fuels outright. Skeptical of mandates and subsidy levels, and protective of energy-sector jobs.

Democrats

Typical view

Strongly support the clean-grid target and home rebates. Some are wary of nuclear and of permitting reform they fear weakens environmental review.

Independents

Typical view

Rob's take

This is the issue where the parties are closest and act like they're farthest apart. The right wants nuclear and faster permits; the left wants clean power and home upgrades. That's the same bill. We build everything clean, we keep the lights on, we cut the cost — and we put the workers who powered the last century in charge of building the next one.

04

Education & Health

Learn for free. Heal without fear.

Knowledge and care are the floor a free society stands on. If you're willing to learn a trade or a field, the price of admission shouldn't be a decade of debt. And no family should ration insulin or skip a doctor because of what it costs. This plan makes skills free, caps the prices that bankrupt people, and gives every kid a real shot from pre-K on.

The Plan

  • Tuition-free community & technical college. Every American can earn a degree or a credential — coding, nursing, welding, electrical — without taking on debt.
  • Cap the prices that break families. Insulin and inhalers at $5; a hard annual out-of-pocket cap on medical costs; transparent, posted prices everywhere.
  • A teacher pay floor. No teacher earns below the local cost of a decent living. Pay the profession like the essential job it is.
  • Universal pre-K and after-school. Treat early education and after-school care as the public service it already is for working parents.
  • Let Medicare negotiate everything. Negotiate drug prices across the board and let the savings cut premiums — the same way every other rich country does it.

How it works

Why community college, not all college

It's the highest-return, lowest-cost rung — credentials that lead straight to good jobs. Free community and technical college covers the majority of workers at a fraction of "free four-year" cost.

~$30B / yr

How price caps work

Caps apply to a defined list of essential drugs and to total out-of-pocket exposure. Negotiation and transparency bring list prices down so caps aren't just shifting costs around.

Net saver

Funding the pay floor

Federal matching grants top up local teacher pay to the cost-of-living floor, targeted first at the districts that struggle most to hire.

~$25B / yr

Pre-K capacity

Funds flow to states to build seats and pay early educators a living wage, with mixed public, private, and faith-based providers — parents choose.

~$35B / yr

Across the aisle

Republicans

Typical view

Open to vocational and technical training and to price transparency, and like that schools stay locally run. Resist price controls and broad new entitlements, and prefer choice-based approaches.

Democrats

Typical view

Champion free college, drug-price negotiation, and universal pre-K. Many would go further — toward free four-year college and a public insurance option.

Independents

Typical view

Rob's take

It's the targeted, payable version of the goal both sides actually share: that a kid's zip code and a family's bank balance shouldn't decide whether they get to learn and stay healthy.
